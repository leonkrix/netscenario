# D-098: The engine is a process in the fabric namespace of a run, with packet sockets on the veth ends, in safe Rust but for one small module, and its timer is chosen by `auto` and can always be set

Accepted (2026-11-12), for BKD-06. Measured by S21 and S23 on Debian 13 (kernel 6.12, Ryzen 7 5800X) in an unprivileged user namespace; the runners of the CI are measured by the workflow `Spikes` (input `only_engine`) and the numbers are added to the log of the plan.

- **The shape.** One engine process for a run, in the fabric namespace of D-079: `node A -- veth -- engine -- veth -- node B`, a packet socket (`AF_PACKET`) on each veth end. Nodes are ordinary namespaces, so routing, TCP, IPv6, fragments, programs and capture are the kernel's and are not rebuilt. The supervisor, the crash guarantee (D-022), `clean` and the limits are those of the Kernel backend; only the middle of a link differs (the engine where `netem` and the shaper were). The engine needs no kernel module and no root, so its tests also run where `netem` is missing.
- **The data path** is one thread for all links of a process (or one thread per link on request): non-blocking sockets, a heap of frames ordered by the moment they are due with the arrival number as tie-break (so a jitter can reorder, and equal moments keep their order), and a timer. S21: 1,000 to 100,000 packets per second of 200 bytes, delay 20 ms, 0 packets lost up to 50,000 per second with every timer and up to 400 of 400,000 lost at 100,000 per second with the sleeping timers (none with the spinning ones); a uniform jitter of 5 ms reordered the packets (99,573 of 200,000 left out of arrival order) and gave the mean, the minimum and the standard deviation of the model (20.0, 15.0 and 2.89 ms); one TCP flow through the engine reached 2.0 to 2.4 Gbit/s against 4.5 Gbit/s on a plain veth with one segment per frame, which is far more than a test needs.
- **The timer, measured as the lateness of a release** (the time between the moment a packet was due and the moment it was handed to the interface), delay 20 ms, 10,000 packets per second: `epoll_pwait2` with a nanosecond timeout (through `rustix`) has a median of 9 to 10 microseconds, a 99th percentile of 0.26 to 0.5 ms and rare outliers of 4 to 7 ms (a core that sleeps wakes late; the load of this desktop shows in it), 2 % of a CPU at 1,000 packets per second, 6 % at 10,000, 30 % at 50,000 and 41 % at 100,000; a timer with a millisecond timeout is 1 ms late at low rates and is not used; `timerfd` is like `epoll_pwait2` and a little later; a spinning loop has a 99th percentile of 5 to 8 microseconds and a maximum of 0.1 ms at the price of a whole CPU; **spin-sleep** (sleep until 100 microseconds before the due moment, then spin) has a 99th percentile of 5 to 13 microseconds, as tight as the kernel (its standard deviation of the delay is 1 to 2 microseconds, the engine's 3), at 6 % of a CPU at 1,000 packets per second and 63 % at 10,000 and more. Its weakness is a low rate: at 1,000 packets per second it sleeps for most of 20 ms and still woke late now and then (99.9th percentile 2.6 ms, maximum 4 ms), because the window of 100 microseconds is shorter than the late wake-ups of this machine. The window therefore adapts: the engine measures how late its sleeps end and spins for that long (the step of the data path builds and tests it). The kernel's own delay is tighter than any timer of the engine; the engine adds 6 to 12 microseconds of base delay for its hop (S23).
- **The timer is a setting, and its default is `auto`.** The engine always measures the lateness of every release and puts the distribution and an overload flag in the result (ENV-08). `engine.timer` is `auto`, `epoll`, `spin-sleep` or `spin`, set with `--engine-timer`, `engine_timer=` and the plugin option, and recorded. `auto` takes spin-sleep when the run has at least 4 CPUs available (by the CPU mask of the process) and `epoll` otherwise, and the result says which and why. The reason: a test author on a laptop or a 4-core runner gets the exact timing the release is named for; a 2-core runner, where a spinning engine would take a core from the sender, the receiver and the programs, gets the cheap timer and an honest report of the lateness. The threshold is one constant, to be confirmed with the numbers of the runners. The setting changes the precision of the timing and never what the engine decides (D-099).
- **`unsafe`.** Everything is safe Rust (`nix`, `rustix`: receive, send, `epoll`, `timerfd`, the clock, the CPU time) but one module of the engine crate, `sys`, about 35 lines: the `bind` of a packet socket to an interface and the two options `PACKET_IGNORE_OUTGOING` (the engine must not read what it sends) and `PACKET_VNET_HDR`. `nix` 0.31.3 cannot build the address that `bind` and `send` need (the field of `LinkAddr` is private and there is no constructor) and neither `nix` nor `rustix` knows the options; there is no safe way today. The module has a `SAFETY` comment for each call and a test, the crate has `#![deny(unsafe_code)]` with the one allowed module, and every other crate keeps `forbid`. A pull request to `nix` was considered and not made (the engine would wait on someone else's release).
- **Checksums, solved without a tool.** A frame that leaves a node over a veth with checksum offload on carries an unfinished checksum; a packet socket that re-sends it hands the receiver a frame it drops (S21: a TCP connection through the engine with the offloads on never opens). `PACKET_VNET_HDR` keeps the unfinished checksum unfinished: each frame is read and written with a 10-byte header that says where to complete it, and the interface that receives it completes it, as from a real wire. TCP through the engine with it (and one segment per frame, D-086) ran at 2.1 Gbit/s; with the transmit offload switched off by the `ethtool` ioctl instead it ran at 2.0 to 2.4 Gbit/s. So no `ethtool` and no extra netlink message is needed, and the engine does not depend on a setting of the nodes.
- **The binary.** The engine is a program, `netscenario-engine`, installed next to the agent (the wheel carries it too, as the agent), started by the supervisor in the fabric namespace; the control channel is lines of JSON, like the agent's.

Alternatives: TAP devices (the program would talk to a TAP, the nodes would no longer be ordinary namespaces and the kernel's routing would not be used); one process per link (more processes, no gain at test rates); XDP or eBPF (BKD-13, not rootless); `epoll` or spin-sleep as the only choice (one is cheap and loose, the other exact and costly: neither is right for every user); a pull request to `nix` first (waiting for a release that is not ours).
