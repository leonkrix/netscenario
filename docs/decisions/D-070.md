# D-070: The series of a workload is by time bin, in two views, in a file of its own, and the time to recover is defined once and says so in the result

Accepted (2026-11-02). PRB-07 asks for the time a workload takes to be back after an event, and PRB-05 for the packets over time. Both need the same thing: the sent, arrived and lost packets in stretches of time that line up with the steps of the timeline.

- **Two views in one table.** A packet has a send moment and an arrival moment, and the events of the timeline live in both frames: a `set`, a `down` and an `up` are done at a packet of the sender (D-062), an `outage` is a window at the delivery (D-064). So each bin holds `by_send` (the packets whose slot lies in the bin: sent, received, lost, ratio, delay) and `by_arrival` (the packets that arrived in it: received, bits, delay), with the same bounds. A series with one of them would answer one of the two questions and leave the other to be guessed from a link delay the reader would have to know.
- **The origin is the start of the measured phase**, the zero of `events[].scheduled_ns`, and not the first packet (the older `throughput` counts from there). Bin 7 is then the bin of a step at 7.3 s, whatever `start` the workload has. The slot of a packet is its nominal moment (`start + offset(n)`), so a late sender does not move a packet into another bin and the loss of a bin is the same in every run of the same seed. A refused packet (D-064) is neither sent nor lost.
- **A file of its own.** `series-<id>.jsonl`, one line for each bin, always written; `result.json` has `series` (`bin_ns`, `bins`, `file`) and the `recoveries`. A result stays small and readable (a 10 minute run in bins of 100 ms is 6,000 lines), and a reader who wants the series opens the file (`Workload.series.read()` in Python). The bound is a million bins for a run and a bin of at least a millisecond, checked before the network is built.
- **The parameters belong to the run, not to the scenario** (changed by [D-073](D-073.md): they are keys of the section `measure` now, and the options of the run replace them for one run): the bin, and the basis, baseline, window and fraction of the recovery were thought to be about how a result is read. The argument against moving them was that a scenario file does not change meaning when somebody wants half-second bins; the argument that won is that the number of a recovery time is not comparable without them.
- **The definition of the time to recover** (written in `RESULTS.md`): the level of a bin is the share of what was sent in it that was delivered; the baseline is the median of the levels of the five bins before the bin of the disturbance (leaving out bins without sending); the level to reach is 90 % of it; the workload has recovered at the start of the first window of three bins, starting at the bin of the event, whose summed deliveries over the summed sends reach the level. The time is that start minus the moment of the event, never negative; its resolution is one bin. Every parameter is written in the entry, so a number is read without knowing the defaults. A median, so one odd bin does not move the baseline; a window, so a bin that flickers back up does not count; the bin of the event as the start, so a loss in the same bin as the event still counts as part of the dip.
- **Which events.** Every `set`, `down`, `up` and the end of an outage, for the workloads they concern (its link and its direction, or the whole link). The start of an outage has none, its end has. The disturbance of an outage's end begins at its start, and that of an `up` at the last `down` of the link; for a `set` it is the event itself, which makes the baseline of a `set` that lifts a limit the degraded level (the answer is 0 s). That is the definition working as written, and `RESULTS.md` says that the recovery to read is that of the event that did the damage.
- **The basis is chosen by the caller.** `send` (default): of the packets sent in the bin, how many got through; it sees a loss where it was caused and does not see a queue. `arrival`: of the packets sent in the bin, how many did the receiver get in it, capped at the packets sent in the bin so that the surplus of a queue that empties does not make up for the bins before it. Neither is right for every question, and the entry says which one it used.
- **Found on the way, measured.** `netem` works out the moment a packet leaves when it enters the queue. A scene with 390 packets waiting at 1 mbit when the rate was lifted to 4 mbit kept the receiver at 120 packets a second for the 3 s the old queue needed, and everything sent in between left after it: with an outage at 9 s, every packet whose delivery fell into the window was lost, 851 of 3,500, although the rate had been back at its value for 3 s. The scene of the test has a queue of 212 packets that is gone 1.47 s after the rate returns. It is in `COVERAGE.md` as something a reader may not expect.
- **Tested.** Unit tests on series with known answers: the two views differ by exactly the delay (canary: a series that counted by arrival only fails), a loss belongs to the bin it was sent in, a refused packet is neither sent nor lost, late starts, the nearest rank of a bin, a smaller bin; for the recovery: the baseline taken before the start of an outage and not before its end, a flickering level does not count (canary for the window), a series that never recovers, the exact fraction (`>=`), an `up` paired with its `down`, an event with no baseline before it, the two bases differing, a surplus of arrivals not counted twice, and the refusal of parameters that make no sense. In the core with the fake backend: the series file adds up to the statistics, wrong options are refused before anything is built, only the steps that concern a workload give a recovery. In real namespaces: a link of 4 mbit with 50 ms of delay and 250 packets a second, the rate at 1.2 mbit from 3 s to 5 s and an outage from 9 s to 11 s: the bits that arrive in the drop, the delays that grow, nothing lost in the queue, more arrivals than sends in the bin after it, no arrival in the bin inside the outage, the lost packets in the bins of the sends before the window, and the recovery after the drop (0 s by `send`, 3 s by `arrival`). Every bound is derived in the test from the rate, the delay and the recorded lateness of the steps; the test passed three times under 24 busy loops on 4 cores.

Alternatives: the series only in `result.json` (a long run with small bins would make the result unreadable); one view only (see above); the bin and the recovery in the scenario file (a way of reading is not part of what is run, and the same scenario would hash differently); a recovery from the delay and not the delivery (a delay budget is a threshold the test sets, which is what a test asserts on the series; the recovery asks about delivery, the thing that a lost packet changes for good); an exponentially weighted level (no window to name, but the number cannot be read back from the series by hand).
