# D-038: Events of the timeline are applied between packets, so that the lost packets stay reproducible

Accepted (2026-10-31), for release 0.2. The mechanism is settled by the spike S11, whose figures are below the rule.

A `netem` queue has one random number generator (`sch_netem.c`: `q->prng`). Every packet draws from it in a fixed order (duplication, loss, corruption, reordering, jitter); `loss random` draws only if the loss is not 0; Gilbert-Elliott and the four-state model draw from it and keep a Markov state; a `change` that carries a seed starts the generator again. So the pattern of lost packets is a function of the seed and of *which parameters each packet met*. An event applied at a moment of the clock lands before a different packet in every run, and once a window with a loss of 0 lies in between, even the alignment of the draws moves.

- **The rule.** An event is applied before the first packet that its workload sends at or after the moment of the event. The sender knows its packet clock (packet n leaves at `start + n x gap`, computed in integers, D-025). Right after it has sent the packet before, it asks the run for the event (`barrier e`), the run has the backend apply the change, and at the slot of the packet the sender goes on (`go e`), waiting only if the answer is not there yet. For the packets of that workload this is the same as an ideal event at the moment, and it does not depend on the scheduling noise of the machine. The packet that waits leaves late by the time of the change (`max_lateness_ns` records it); the packets after it keep their absolute schedule and the late ones are sent at once, in the right order.
- **The guarantee.** Same seed, same events, same lost packets, if one workload sends into the direction and nothing else enters the queue (the counter of D-036 checks the number).
- **What stays not reproducible, and says so with the reason in the result and on the command line:** events of a direction that no workload of the scenario sends into (a program of your own sends there; the events are applied by the clock, S11 measures how late); a second workload in the same direction (the order in which their packets reach the queue cannot be fixed on a real kernel; this holds in release 0.1 as well, where the result said `true`, which D-065 corrects); jitter, as before (D-030).
- **Recorded:** every event with the moment it was applied and the number of packets that had entered the queue.
- **Measured (S11, 2026-10-31, Debian 13, kernel 6.12, 16 processors, 20 runs for each case, the machine idle and with every processor busy; `spikes/rust-timeline/`).**
  - *How a change is applied.* A `tc -batch -` that lives for the whole run (one for each node, started through `nsenter`) takes 0.026 ms for a change (p50; p99 0.05 ms idle and 1.4 ms busy). One `tc` process for each change takes 1.0 ms idle and 3.6 ms busy, and the `apply` of release 0.1 starts two processes (`nsenter`, then `tc`). The kernel has the change when the line `qdisc show dev <interface>` that follows it in the batch has printed. That line must not say `root`: `tc` keeps the parent between the lines of a batch, so the second `show ... root` fails and ends the batch. A `change` without a `seed` keeps the generator, so the seed that was set at the start stays in force.
  - *The barrier is exact.* The change landed before the intended packet in 20 of 20 runs for every combination that was run (a persistent batch idle and busy, a process each time idle). A `send` that has returned has put the packet into the queue of the interface, so a change that is applied after it is applied before the next packet. The same seed gave the same lost packets in 10 of 10 runs for six kinds of change (loss 0 to 30 %, 10 to 30 %, 30 to 0 to 30 %, an outage of 30 to 100 to 30 %, a duplication switched on), idle and busy.
  - *The clock is not.* A timer that applies the change at the moment of the packet landed one packet late in every idle run (two with a `tc` process each time, because the change itself takes time), and between 1 and 8 packets late with the machine busy. Idle, the lost packets were the same in all ten runs for four of the five kinds with a change (30 to 0 to 30 % differed in one run); busy they differed for all five. Where a window with a loss of 0 lies in between (0 to 30 %, 30 to 0 to 30 %) the whole pattern moved (up to 326 packets differ); where the loss is never 0 (10 to 30 %, an outage) only the packets at the boundary changed (1 to 6). That is the model of this entry: with a loss of 0 a packet draws no number, so the alignment shifts; with any other loss every packet draws exactly one.
  - *What the waiting packet costs.* Asked ahead (the rule above): the packet leaves 0.06 ms late idle with the batch (0.55 ms with a process each time), and busy 0.07 ms at p50, 6.7 ms at p90, when the machine itself delays the change. Asked only at the slot: 0.15 ms idle, 1.2 ms (p50) and 4 ms (max) busy. So the lateness of the packet is the time of the change or the delay of the machine, whichever is larger, and `max_lateness_ns` shows it.
  - *On the runner images* (the workflow `Spikes`, 2026-10-31, Ubuntu 22.04 with kernel 6.8 and Ubuntu 24.04 with kernel 7.0, 4 processors each, `tc` of iproute2 built from source): the picture is the same. (Two runs; the figures below are those of the first, the second gave the same picture with a persistent batch at 0.057 and 0.043 ms, a `tc` process each time at 1.17 and 0.86 ms, and 2.6 ms for a process and 0.05 ms for the batch with all four processors busy; the second ran with the answer timeout and `stdbuf`, 5 PASS and no FAIL on both images.) A persistent batch applies a change in 0.044 ms and 0.037 ms (p50; p99 2.3 ms and 0.8 ms with all four processors busy), a `tc` process each time in 0.97 and 0.78 ms (2.6 and 2.5 ms busy). The barrier landed on the intended packet in 20 of 20 runs in every combination on both images and gave the same lost packets in 10 of 10 runs for every kind of change, idle and busy. The timer was one packet late with the batch and two with a process each time in every idle run on both, and between 0 and 5 (22.04) and 1 and 3 (24.04) late busy; busy, it changed the lost packets for all five kinds on 22.04 and for four of the five on 24.04. The number of lost packets of each series (about 316, 228, 252, 303, 350 and 279) is the same on this machine and on both images. With all four processors busy the packet that waits for a change leaves late by 1 to 2 ms (p50) and up to 7 ms; that is the cost on a loaded runner, and `max_lateness_ns` shows it.
  - *One hang, and what follows from it.* The first run of the workflow waited more than 37 minutes for the first answer of a persistent `tc -batch -` (the `tc` built from source, no message). The second run, in which the spike starts `tc` under `stdbuf -oL` and waits at most 5 seconds for an answer, worked on both images. The cause was not seen (the difference may be that this `tc` holds its output back in a pipe; with the `tc` 6.15 of this machine the answer comes at once even when the output is fully buffered). So the supervisor starts its batch the same way, gives every change a time limit, and a batch that does not answer is replaced by a `tc` process for that change (slower, still exact), with a line in the result.
  - *Not measured:* the events of a direction without a workload (a program of your own), which are applied by the clock: the timer woke 0.06 ms late idle and up to 4 ms busy, plus the time of the change.
- **Exact for everything** (programs, several workloads, any timing) is what the Engine backend of release 0.4 is for: it runs on virtual time.

Alternatives: events by the clock with the statement "not reproducible" (simple, and it gives up the property that the project puts first); packet counts as the only trigger (no meaning in seconds); the loss decided in the sender by a function of the sequence number (exact, but then the impairment is not in the network, and a program's own traffic has no sequence number); a BPF program in the queue (needs rights that the unprivileged way does not have).
